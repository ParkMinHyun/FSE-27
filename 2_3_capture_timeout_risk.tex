\subsection{Challenges}
\label{sec:capture-timeout-risk}
\label{sec:guard-limit}

Enabling MFC in the draft sequence under parallel capture has caused frequent Capture Timeout violations during pre-release validation.
Although parallel capture allows consecutive capture requests to overlap, their draft sequences execute sequentially on a single draft worker.
For each accepted capture request, the camera collects the required input frames and metadata before enqueueing the corresponding draft sequence. The sequence may then wait in the queue while the capture's timeout clock continues to run.
Figure~\ref{fig:overlap} illustrates how successive captures can have progressively less time to execute their draft sequences, even when all draft sequences take equally long.
In particular, capture $i+2$ exceeds its deadline because the preceding captures consume much of its timeout budget through queueing.
The additional processing required for MFC can further increase queueing delays and cause more Capture Timeout violations.
Consequently, Samsung has not deployed MFC in the draft sequence of commercial devices, including the Galaxy S26 series.

\myparagraph{Current Strategy}
Even without MFC, Capture Timeout violations can occur under parallel capture, particularly in camera modes with computationally intensive draft sequences.
To mitigate these violations, Samsung uses a static thermal guard that skips optional image-processing stages (e.g., filter and watermark processing) in the draft sequence when the overheat level reaches 4 or higher.
Samsung's overheat level is a thermal indicator ranging from 0 to 6, with higher levels indicating stronger throttling.
However, the overheat level does not account for the time already spent waiting in the draft queue or the processing work remaining in the draft sequence.
Therefore, the thermal guard cannot reliably determine whether additional processing, including MFC, can complete within a capture's remaining timeout budget.


\myparagraph{Motivating experiment}
To examine the timeout risk of enabling lightweight multi-frame composition in the draft sequence, we compared two Portrait configurations on the Galaxy S26 Ultra.
We bypassed the thermal guard so that configured optional stages could execute regardless of the overheat level.
The optional stages are the multi-frame composition stage (\(M\)) and the deployed single-frame stages (collectively \(S\)).
Both \(M\) alone and \(M+S\) retain the same mandatory encoding and saving stages.
We tested 12MP and 24MP capture under normal memory conditions and under memory pressure.
For each condition in Table~\ref{tab:timeout_index}, we ran ten trials of up to 30 consecutive captures.
We chose 30 captures to extend testing beyond the earlier level-4 validation failure near the 20th consecutive capture.
The table reports the earliest timeout capture index across the ten trials, grouped by starting overheat level.

With the thermal guard bypassed, \(M+S\) timed out in 13 of the 16 conditions with starting levels below the guard's cutoff.
Every \(M+S\) trial starting at level~4 or higher nevertheless completed its first capture without a timeout, even though the guard would have skipped the optional stages for that capture.
Even at the same starting level, the earliest timeout varied with resolution, memory pressure, and draft configuration.
In conditions where \(M+S\) timed out, reducing optional work to \(M\) alone delayed the earliest timeout or resulted in no observed timeout within 30 captures.
Together, these observations motivate runtime control of optional-stage execution based on each capture's remaining timeout budget and draft workload, rather than the overheat level alone.
