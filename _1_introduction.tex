\section{Introduction}

\label{sec:introduction}

The Samsung Galaxy camera publishes an initial user-visible image, the \emph{draft image}, for each capture and later replaces it with the final image from post-processing.
Samsung refers to the processing stages that produce the draft image as the \emph{draft sequence}.
For certain shot modes, post-processing is deferred until the user switches away from the camera application.
Until then, the draft image remains the user-visible result, making its visual quality more important.
To narrow the draft--final visual gap, Samsung has already deployed single-frame stages in the draft sequence and implemented lightweight multi-frame composition to reduce the gap further.

However, the added processing of lightweight multi-frame composition can violate the \emph{Capture Timeout}, a fixed per-capture deadline for producing the draft image.
This deadline becomes harder to meet under parallel capture, which shortens shot-to-shot intervals by accepting the next shutter input before the preceding draft image is produced.
Yet draft sequences still execute one at a time on a single draft worker, so later captures may wait in the draft queue while their timeout clocks keep running.
Added work in earlier draft sequences therefore lengthens this wait and can cause queued captures to miss their deadlines.
Since Capture Timeout violations crash the application and block release, multi-frame composition could not be deployed in draft sequences.

Even for the deployed single-frame draft stages, Samsung uses a static thermal guard to mitigate timeout risk.
The guard disables optional single-frame stages at overheat level~4 or higher on a 0--6 scale.
However, with composition enabled and the guard bypassed, timeouts occurred in 13 of 16 S26 Ultra settings starting below the cutoff.
At the same starting level, the first timeout occurred at different capture indices depending on resolution, stage configuration, and memory pressure (Table~\ref{tab:timeout_index}).
The overheat level reflects neither how much timeout budget a capture has left nor how much draft work remains.
Deploying multi-frame composition under parallel capture therefore requires runtime control based on remaining timeout budgets and the workload of the draft sequence.

Prior work in mobile and server systems has studied workload control~\cite{approxdet-sensys,timecard-sosp,klein-brownout} and arrival control~\cite{seda-sosp,dagor-socc,breakwater-osdi} to manage latency.
However, the Samsung camera's draft pipeline can neither reject an accepted capture nor preempt a running stage.
Skipping optional stages reduces remaining processing time but cannot recover timeout budget already consumed by queueing.
In contrast, delaying the next capture opportunity allows the queue to drain, leaving subsequent captures more timeout budget for processing.
The two controls are coupled through the single draft worker.
Admitted optional work increases the backlog that pacing must accommodate, while pacing preserves budget for optional processing in subsequent captures.

In this paper, we present the Budget-Aware Draft Controller, which coordinates these two controls to enable lightweight multi-frame composition in the draft sequence within per-capture deadlines.
A major challenge is deciding how much time to reserve for draft work: too little risks a Capture Timeout, whereas too much needlessly suppresses optional processing or prolongs shot-to-shot intervals.
To address this, \emph{Remaining-Sequence Admission} uses recent duration residuals to calibrate an empirical upper estimate for the entire remaining draft sequence and admits an optional stage when this estimate fits within the capture's remaining timeout budget.
\emph{Capture-Availability Pacing} delays the next capture opportunity when projected draft workload exceeds the remaining timeout budget of the most recently enqueued capture.
Both use a shared duration predictor updated from measured durations in the current camera session, without per-device timing tables.

Across 28 settings spanning two devices, two capture conditions, and seven starting overheat levels, with the guard bypassed, the controller completed all 280 runs without Capture Timeout, whereas uncontrolled execution of all optional stages timed out in 225 runs.
Multi-frame composition executed on an average of at least 4.5 of the first five captures per run in every setting, including those starting at or above the guard's cutoff.
With production-default 12MP capture under normal memory conditions, pacing did not delay the first five captures at starting overheat levels below 4.
Only coordinated control completed all captures on time in all three ablation settings, while executing multi-frame composition 1.3--2.0 times as often as admission alone.
The controller is integrated into the product branch and is undergoing release validation for a forthcoming Galaxy flagship line.

\smallskip\noindent\textbf{Contributions.}
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.2em]
  \item We characterize how serialized draft execution under parallel capture creates a cross-capture deadline problem for lightweight multi-frame composition, and why a static thermal cutoff cannot determine per-capture deadline safety.
  \item We present the Budget-Aware Draft Controller, which coordinates remaining-sequence admission with capture-availability pacing. To our knowledge, this is the first work to combine these controls in production camera software under a hard per-capture deadline.
  \item We evaluate the controller on two commercial devices, examining end-to-end effectiveness, component contribution, admission decision quality against naive approaches, and pacing under queue pressure.
\end{itemize}
